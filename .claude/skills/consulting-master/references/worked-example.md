# Worked example: motor insurance renewal rate decline (fictional)

All companies and numbers are fictional and illustrative.

**1. Frame.** Client: a non-life insurer. Private-car motor renewal rate fell from 80% (full-year baseline) to 74% (latest half-year), down 6 percentage points. The client's contact wants to decide whether to fund more claims staff and online damage claims next quarter. Claims team believes slow claims are the cause; sales believes competitors are cheaper.

**2. Validate the metric.** Renewal counted within how many days after expiry? Same definition across channels? Any portfolio clean-up (non-renewal by the insurer) inside the number? Claims days counted from report date or document receipt?

**3. Problem statement (initial).** Private-car motor renewal fell from 80% to 74% (−6 points) across all channels, weakening premium income and customer base stability. Views differ on the cause. Within six weeks, identify and quantify the main drivers so that leadership can decide where to invest, aiming to restore renewal to at least 78% within two renewal cycles.

**4. MECE structure.** Non-renewal = insurer-side non-renewal + customer-side lapse. Customer-side: claims experience · renewal price change (experience rating and general rate change) · renewal process (notice, payment) · channel service · non-price competition · customer circumstances (sold or replaced car). Boundary rule: claims experience is compared only among customers with similar premium change; price effect is measured mainly on customers without claims. Cuts: channel, region, vehicle segment.

**5. Localize.** Decline concentrated where? Start date? Coincides with the rate change and a notice-channel switch to e-notices.

**6. Hypotheses (alternatives tree).** H1 claims experience: more insured with claims over 15 days, and they renew less. H2 price: more insured with renewal premium up 10% or more, and they renew less. H3 process: after e-notices, those who did not open renew less.

**7. Prioritize.** H2 first: high on impact, likelihood and speed, and it is needed as a control for H1. H3 is cheaper to test than it looks and gets a quick check.

**8. Analysis plan with pass/fail set in advance.**
- H1: within equal premium-change bands, compare renewal of claims ≤10 days vs >15 days. Pass if the gap is at least 5 points and holds in every band.
- H2: renewal by premium-change band; repeat on customers without claims. Pass if the ≥10% band is at least 8 points below the no-increase band and holds without claims.
- H3: opened vs not opened e-notice. Pass if the gap is at least 5 points and the not-opened share exceeds last year's non-response to paper notices.

**9. Illustrative findings.** H2 supported: the ≥10% band grew from 12% to 25% of policies and renews at about 60% vs about 82%. H1 supported: long claims grew from 4% to 10% of policies with a gap of about 20 points. H3 rejected: gap of 2 points.

**10. Contributions and recommendation.**

| Driver | Method | Contribution | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price (H2) | (25% − 12%) × (82% − 60%) | about 2.9 points | about 48% |
| Claims experience (H1) | (10% − 4%) × 20 points | about 1.2 points | about 20% |
| Other / unexplained | residual | about 1.9 points | about 32% |
| Total | | 6.0 points | 100% |

Call: Modify. Narrow the claims investment to cases over 15 days rather than broad hiring; ask actuarial and underwriting to review renewal loadings, noting that lower loadings can raise the loss ratio because price must stay commensurate with risk; run a lapse-reason survey to explain the 32% residual (customer circumstances, competitor offers).

**Lesson.** The client framed the decision around claims investment. Listing alternatives and prioritizing by evidence moved the recommendation from "fund claims" to "narrow claims investment and review renewal pricing".
